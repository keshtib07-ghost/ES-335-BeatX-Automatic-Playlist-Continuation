Research,
in plain words.

We generate playlists from a name and a few seed songs. We ask whether more seeds improve recommendations and whether title context helps.

Start here for the essentials. The Deep Dive keeps every method explanation, graph, error case, limitation and reproduction step.

01 / How it works

Your songs supply the starting point.

Three seeds seen in training

  1. Find related songs. Item-based collaborative filtering (CF) finds songs that shared training playlists with your seeds. Cosine similarity adjusts for popularity; shrinkage reduces trust in rare pairs.
  2. Combine the evidence. Add neighbour scores across seeds, excluding the seeds themselves. Each seed contributes up to 200 neighbours.
  3. Use context and filters. Title rules and a text classifier infer tags. Song-tag associations provide context; selected tags, BPM and era constrain eligibility. A configured context boost can adjust the CF order.
  4. Return the playlist. Return up to 20 additions, with fewer when evidence or filters limit the options. Playback flow can reorder them; Spotify export saves them.

Some seeds are unfamiliar

Use any seeds with training evidence. Unknown seeds add no learned similarity. If none is usable, rank popular training songs using the title and selected context. If the title is vague too, fall back to training popularity, subject to active filters.

The model does not listen to an unfamiliar seed or learn its properties during the request. Its recommendations still come from training-known tracks.

Title context guides ranking; explicit filters control eligibility. In the unified code, a missing settings file means a zero context boost, so a title does not always change seed-based ordering.

02 / What we learned from

One million existing playlists.

Spotify’s Million Playlist Dataset provides song identities, titles and playlist membership—not trusted audio mood, BPM or bass measurements.

Training learns relationships; validation chooses settings; test evaluates frozen choices. We split playlists, so a song can occur in both training and test. Repeated track IDs within a playlist are removed, and exact track-set duplicates share a split.

Why do we also mention 60,000 titles?

CF uses all 804,485 training playlists. Only the auxiliary title classifier is fitted on up to 60,000 sampled training titles, using word/character TF-IDF and logistic regression. It learns weak labels supplied by keyword rules. The context pipeline then scores all training playlists to build song associations, combining duplicate groups. The title cap is configurable; no published study proves 60k optimal.

The staged data contains 65,464,776 distinct memberships; 881,652 repeated occurrences were removed. There are 2,032,356 training-known tracks. The older benchmark uses 12 context tags; expanded Studio profiles use 19. Full training breakdown →

Title context adds a small overall gain: +0.00165 NDCG, with a positive paired confidence interval. Only 660/5,000 titles (13.2%) receive tags.

More seeds helped.

Experiment B compares 1, 2, 3, 5, 10 and 20 seeds on 1,500 validation and 1,500 fresh test queries, keeping each query’s references fixed. Learned-title test NDCG rises from 0.1284 to 0.3142.

A validation-only rule selects 20 seeds as the smallest tested budget reaching 95% of peak validation NDCG. This is the largest budget tested; we did not establish an early plateau or a universal optimum.

Context did not always help.

Keyword and learned-title methods are very close in B. At 20 seeds, title reranking improves 84 queries, worsens 70 and leaves 1,346 unchanged. The tagged-query interval includes zero.

Recovery can improve while catalogue coverage falls. Artist variety, catalogue reach and popularity bias describe different tradeoffs; none directly measures listening satisfaction.

A and B use different cohorts and reference protocols. Compare methods within each experiment. Neither supplies performance results for the newer unified Studio ranker.

What else did we measure?

A’s tagged-query NDCG rises from 0.20409 to 0.21662. The report also includes Precision@20, hit rate, paired bootstrap intervals, marginal seed gains and actual diagnostic cases. B reserves 20 possible seeds; all other tracks form the fixed references. Unrevealed reserved seeds earn no recovery credit.

A separate order study holds the same 20 known songs and first song fixed. Training-transition ordering recovers 15.61% of original directed adjacencies versus 5.57% for a seeded shuffle, across 401 eligible sequences. 04 / What these results do not establish

Useful clues, with limits.

  • Tags describe grouping. A Gym tag reflects workout-like playlist titles. Listeners did not verify workout suitability; titles can mislead, and sparse evidence may leave songs untagged.
  • Unseen tracks remain a weakness. In A, 5,922 of 316,437 withheld occurrences (1.87%) are absent from training. They stay in the scoring denominator. Seven queries have all three seeds unseen.
  • Offline recovery is only one test. Existing playlists are not a complete list of good recommendations or explicit dislikes. Exact Spotify IDs can also separate alternate releases of the same recording.
  • New features need their own evidence. Unified Studio behaviour is implemented, but its evaluation outputs are not published here. ALS/BPR remain untested in the full-run comparison; no reported ablation establishes a BPM, era, bass or treble gain.

Next steps are reviewed-label and retrieval audits, controlled title-training budgets, additional model and enrichment ablations, and a fresh listener study. Select changes on validation before using a fresh final test.
