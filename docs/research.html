How many seed songs are needed?

How does the number of seed songs affect the quality of generated playlists, and what is the minimum number of seed songs required to achieve effective recommendations?

Does playlist context help?

Does incorporating mood information and musical features into collaborative filtering improve playlist recommendation quality compared with traditional collaborative filtering methods?

What counts as a prediction?

One query is a held-out playlist. The user supplies visible seed songs and optional context. The model returns up to 20 additions. Hidden songs are used only to score the result.

Data unit
One playlist; each matrix entry records whether a song belongs to it.
Prediction time
After the request, before new songs are selected.
Available information
The request and frozen training evidence.
Claim
Recovery of hidden songs. Listening satisfaction is not measured.

01 / How it works

How BeatX recommends songs

When the model recognises your seeds

  1. Find related songs. Collaborative filtering (CF) finds songs that appeared in playlists with your seeds. It adjusts for song popularity and gives less weight to pairs seen together only a few times.
  2. Combine the seed scores. Each seed supplies up to 200 related songs. We add their scores and exclude the seeds from the new recommendations.
  3. Apply context. The title and seed profiles suggest context tags. Your selected filters determine which songs are allowed. A configured context weight can also change their order.
  4. Return the results. Recommend up to 20 additions. Strict filters can produce fewer. Playback flow changes their order; Spotify export puts your seeds first, followed by the recommendations.

When seeds are missing from training

Use the seeds the model knows. If it knows none, use the title and selected filters to rank popular songs from training. If the title yields no usable context either, use popularity alone. Active filters still apply.

The model cannot learn the sound of an unfamiliar seed from its name. Its recommendations come from songs seen during training.

We learn from the training playlists, choose settings on validation, then evaluate the fixed model on test playlists. A song may appear in more than one split. Repeated track IDs within a playlist count once, and playlists containing exactly the same track set stay together.

CF uses all 804,485 training playlists. Both text classifiers use all their titles. The benchmark classifier predicts 12 tags; Studio predicts 19 and rebuilds each song’s context profile from the training playlists. Training details →

03 / What the experiments found

What the experiments found

For each test playlist, show the model a few songs and hide the rest. Recall@20 measures the fraction of hidden songs recovered in its first 20 recommendations. NDCG@20 also rewards placing those songs near the top.

Adding title context raises NDCG@20 by 0.00160. The paired 95% confidence interval is 0.00107 to 0.00212. The classifier finds usable tags for 657 of 5,000 titles.

More seeds improved recovery through 20

A separate study tested six seed counts on 1,500 validation playlists and 1,500 fresh test playlists. Each playlist kept the same hidden songs for every seed count. Test NDCG@20 for CF with learned title tags rose from 0.1284 at one seed to 0.3142 at 20.

We chose the smallest tested seed count reaching 95% of the best validation NDCG. That rule selected 20 seeds, the largest count tested. We therefore cannot say that five or ten seeds are enough, or that 20 is optimal for every user.

Title context sometimes made results worse

The keyword and learned-title methods performed similarly in the seed-count study. With 20 seeds, learned title tags improved 84 playlists, worsened 70 and left 1,346 unchanged. The confidence interval for playlists with tagged titles includes zero, so that comparison does not establish an average gain.

Better recovery can come with lower catalogue coverage: the model finds more hidden songs while recommending fewer distinct tracks across the test sample. Artist variety and popularity bias measure other effects. We have not measured listening satisfaction.

Scope: the seed-count study used a different title model and test sample from the three-seed benchmark. The live Studio adds 19-tag profiles and different filtering rules. Its ranking quality still needs a separate published evaluation.

Additional results and evaluation details

In the three-seed benchmark, NDCG@20 for playlists with tagged titles rises from 0.20355 to 0.21572. The full report also gives precision and hit rate, with confidence intervals and saved failure cases. In the seed-count study, 20 songs are reserved as possible seeds. All other songs form the fixed hidden set; recommending an unused reserved seed earns no credit.

A separate ordering study keeps the same 20 known songs and the first song fixed. Across 401 eligible playlists, learned transitions recover 15.61% of original next-song pairs, compared with 5.57% for a seeded shuffle. This tests recovery of the original order; it does not measure perceived flow. See the ordering results →

04 / Limitations

What the results do not show

  • Tags come from titles. A Gym tag means a song appeared in playlists with workout-related titles. Listeners have not verified its suitability for exercise. Vague titles and limited evidence can produce incorrect or missing tags.
  • Unseen songs cannot be recovered. In the three-seed test, 5,922 of 316,437 hidden song occurrences (1.87%) were absent from training. They still count when we calculate recall. Seven playlists had no seed seen during training.
  • A missed match is not necessarily a bad recommendation. An existing playlist cannot list every suitable song. Alternate releases may also have different Spotify IDs, even when they contain the same recording.
  • Studio’s extra features remain to be evaluated. Its expanded context profiles need a controlled ranking comparison. Separate tests are also needed for the audio and release-date filters.
